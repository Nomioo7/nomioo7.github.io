Compound Interest Explained (With Real Examples)

Updated November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Compound interest is interest earned on your interest. With simple interest, you earn on the original amount only. With compounding, each period's interest is added to the balance, and the next period's interest is calculated on that bigger number. Over long periods, that difference becomes enormous.

The compound interest formula

A = P × (1 + r / n)n × t

  • A: final amount
  • P: starting principal
  • r: annual interest rate as a decimal (6% = 0.06)
  • n: number of times interest compounds per year
  • t: number of years

Worked example: $10,000 at 6% for 10 years

Say you deposit $10,000 at 6% a year, compounded monthly (n = 12), and leave it for 10 years (t = 10).

A = 10,000 × (1 + 0.06 / 12)120 = 10,000 × 1.005120 ≈ $18,193.97

You earned about $8,194 in interest. With simple interest, the same deposit earns only $6,000 (10,000 × 0.06 × 10), finishing at $16,000. Compounding adds almost $2,200 on top, with no extra effort.

Does compounding frequency matter?

Here's the same $10,000 at 6% for 10 years with different compounding schedules:

CompoundingnFinal balanceInterest earned
Annually1$17,908.48$7,908.48
Quarterly4$18,140.18$8,140.18
Monthly12$18,193.97$8,193.97
Daily365$18,220.29$8,220.29

Going from annual to daily compounding adds about $312 over a decade. It helps, but it's small next to the effect of time and rate. That's why the advertised APY, which already includes compounding, is the best number for comparing savings accounts.

Adding monthly contributions

Most people don't deposit once and walk away. They add money regularly. The future value of a regular monthly deposit is:

FV = PMT × [(1 + i)N − 1] / i

where i is the monthly rate (r / 12) and N is the number of months. Saving $200 a month at 6%:

  • After 10 years: you put in $24,000, and the balance is about $32,776.
  • After 30 years: you put in $72,000, and the balance is about $200,903.

Tripling the time nearly multiplies the result by six. That's compounding's real lesson: starting early beats almost everything else.

The Rule of 72

To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the annual rate. At 6%, 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years. At 9%, about 8 years. It's an approximation, but it's accurate enough for quick mental math between roughly 4% and 12%.

Compounding works against you on debt

The same math applies to credit cards and loans. A balance at 20%+ APR that compounds monthly grows fast if you only make minimum payments. Before taking on debt, run the numbers with a loan payment calculator to see the total interest you'll pay over the full term.

Common mistakes

  • Using 6 instead of 0.06 for the rate. Always convert the percentage to a decimal.
  • Mismatching n and t. If you compound monthly, the exponent is months (n × t), not years.
  • Ignoring inflation. A 6% nominal return with 3% inflation is roughly 3% in real buying power.
  • Confusing APR and APY. APY includes compounding, and APR usually doesn't.
