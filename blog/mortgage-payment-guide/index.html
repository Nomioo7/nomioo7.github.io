How Mortgage Payments Are Calculated

Updated November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

A fixed-rate mortgage payment stays the same every month, but what's inside it changes. Early on, most of the payment goes to interest. By the end, almost all of it pays down the loan itself. Once you see the formula, the reason is obvious, and so are the levers that lower your total cost.

The monthly payment formula

M = P × [ r(1 + r)n ] / [ (1 + r)n − 1 ]

  • M: monthly principal and interest payment
  • P: loan amount (home price minus down payment)
  • r: monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12)
  • n: total number of payments (years × 12)

Worked example: $300,000 at 6.5% for 30 years

  1. Monthly rate: r = 0.065 ÷ 12 ≈ 0.0054167
  2. Number of payments: n = 30 × 12 = 360
  3. (1 + r)360 ≈ 6.992
  4. M = 300,000 × (0.0054167 × 6.992) ÷ (6.992 − 1) ≈ $1,896.20

Over 360 payments you pay about $682,633 in total, and $382,633 of that is interest. That's more than the amount you borrowed.

In month one, interest is 300,000 × 0.0054167 = $1,625, so only $271.20 of your first payment reduces the balance. Each month the balance shrinks a little, interest falls, and more of the same payment goes to principal. This schedule is called amortization.

15-year vs 30-year mortgage

Same $300,000 loan, same 6.5% rate, different terms:

The 15-year loan costs about $717 more each month but saves roughly $212,000 in interest. In practice, 15-year loans usually also come with a lower rate, which widens the gap further.

Each percentage point adds or removes roughly $195–$200 per month and about $70,000 over the life of the loan. Shopping several lenders and improving your credit score before applying can be worth more than months of saving.

What your real payment includes (PITI)

The formula gives principal and interest only. Lenders usually collect more each month:

  • Principal and Interest: from the formula above
  • Taxes: property tax, often paid through an escrow account
  • Insurance: homeowners insurance, plus PMI on many conventional loans with less than 20% down

HOA fees, if any, are on top of that.

How much house can you afford?

A common lender guideline is the 28/36 rule: keep housing costs under 28% of gross monthly income, and all debt payments under 36%. On a $7,000 monthly income, that's about $1,960 for housing. A quick percentage calculator makes it easy to check your own ratio.

Ways to pay less interest

  • Make a larger down payment to shrink P and possibly avoid PMI.
  • Pay extra toward principal. Even small extra payments early on cut years off the loan.
  • Choose a shorter term if the higher payment fits your budget.
  • Refinance when rates drop enough to cover the closing costs.
